Chapter 1
Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) has developed from a specialized field of research into a general-purpose technology increasingly integrated into scientific research, business, healthcare, search, and everyday digital applications [26, 52]. Its adoption has expanded rapidly, with 88% of surveyed organizations reporting the use of AI in at least one business function and 70% reporting the use of generative AI in at least one business function [26].  At the same time, advances in model capabilities have allowed modern AI systems to meet or exceed human baselines on several challenging benchmarks, including PhD-level science questions, multimodal reasoning, and competition mathematics [26]. This rapid growth in both adoption and capability shows how AI is becoming increasingly embedded in real-world workflows across different sectors. As these systems become more capable and widely used, ensuring their security, reliability, and safe integration becomes increasingly important.
AI summaries have become commonplace in search engines, with Google’s AI Overview showing up in most of the queried results. Sajadieh et al. [26] reported that AI has already been utilized by medical professionals in healthcare, while in scientific research, “AI models for science can outperform human scientists, though bigger models do not always perform better.” Currently, various AI systems are used by scientists to assist with their workflows, synthesis of literature, and analysis of data. With the introduction of AI agents like Google’s AI co-scientist, researchers are now able to generate, debate, and refine scientific hypotheses with AI, improving efficiency in finishing tasks. For programming, software developers, especially junior-level and less-experienced software developers, have benefited from using GitHub Copilot to increase output [26]. However, Becker et al. [1], in their study on the impact of AI on experienced open-source developer productivity, found that “AI actually increases completion time by 19%, AI tooling slowed developers down.” Finally, in consumers, generative AI adoption reached 53% of the global population within only three years, spreading faster than the personal computer or internet  [26].
This massive growth has been driven in large part by advances in large language models (LLMs) which have rapidly become the dominant paradigm in AI research over the past several years. AI which was previously limited to models that were designed to complete specific tasks, has now evolved to large models that are capable of achieving state-of-the-art results at tasks ranging from simple text translation or summarization all the way to generating code, web design, and critical thinking. This evolution represents an important shift from task-specific architectures toward general-purpose foundation models. Rather than relying on isolated training setups for each application, a single massive foundation model can now generalize across multiple domains through Abstract Representation Learning and Intrinsic Generalization and Transfer.
The same capabilities that have made LLMs so widely available and adopted have also introduced a growing set of security and reliability issues that are not yet fully understood or resolved. Within this current state, security and reliability concerns span several categories at different layers of interaction with the model and its lifecycle deployment [24].  At the user-interaction level, prompt injection and jailbreaking are examples of adversarial exploits that use crafted inputs to manipulate the instruction-following nature of the model and bypass its safety protocols. Prompt injection is when an attacker hides malicious instructions in the prompt or a content that the model processes such as an image or a document, so that the model follows the attacker’s commands instead of its original instructions. Jailbreaking on the other hand, uses prompts to trick a model into ignoring its safety guidelines and providing content it would normally not produce. This vulnerability becomes dangerous because most AI models are tested in normal environments where they perform well, so their weaknesses against adversarial prompts or inputs go unnoticed until after it is deployed. However, their defenses and safety performance weaken under these kinds of attacks [26]. Most safety training instructs the model to recognize and refuse harmful requests that it has seen before. Because the model can’t always tell trusted instructions from the content it is processing, these inputs may override its safeguards. Alongside these are threats to data and privacy, where sensitive training data or uploaded user information is exposed. These threats and vulnerabilities are compounded by weaknesses in fail-safing the system and attacks like data poisoning that corrupt the system before it is even deployed.
Understanding these vulnerabilities highlights why LLM security differs from traditional machine learning security models. In traditional systems, models are usually trained on structured datasets to perform constrained, task-specific roles. In contrast, LLMs operate on unrestricted, natural language inputs and generate context-specific, dynamic outputs, allowing them to generalize across a wide range of tasks without retraining them on task-specific data [24]. Furthermore, training-time data poisoning and backdoor attacks represent one of the most stealthy and high-stakes threats. Data poisoning happens when an attacker inserts manipulated or malicious  examples into the data a model learns from. As the model learns these examples, it can learn unusual behaviors or hidden trigger responses that stay dormant until a specific input activates it. Runtime exploits such as prompt injections can often be monitored at the application level. Backdoors are different, they are ingrained directly into the models during pre-training and fine-tuning in poisoned datasets. This kind of attack is concerning, especially since a backdoor is designed to remain normal during testing, evading detection until triggered by a specific input sequence [34]. Additionally, these cannot be easily identified and cleaned by traditional surface-level techniques, as these backdoor vulnerabilities can survive safety fine-tuning, acting as a “sleeper agent” that executes dangerous outputs when activated  [12]. 
1.1 Project Context
In 2017, Vaswani et al. [31] introduced the transformer architecture which relied entirely on self-attention instead of recurrent or convolutional networks. In this architecture, it improved parallelization and resulted in a more efficient sequence processing. A Transformer is a type of neural network architecture which makes the model look at different words in a sequence and determine how they relate to one another. This architecture later became the foundation for many LLMs. Examples of widely used LLMs today include OpenAI’s GPT models, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude, Meta’s Llama, and DeepSeek. Brown et al. [2] demonstrated that scaling language models could substantially improve their ability to perform a variety of language tasks using only instructions or a small number of examples. 
LLMs today are more capable than before as they can now answer complex questions, summarize long texts, translate languages, help produce code, debug software code, generate content, extract information, and even solve complex mathematical problems. Fang et al. [7] evaluated the mathematical capabilities of LLMs using MathOdyssey, a benchmark that includes high school, university, and Olympiad-level mathematical problems. Their results showed that advanced LLMs can perform well in mathematical reasoning. OpenAI o1-preview achieved the highest overall score of 65.12%. However, the models still had difficulty with the more challenging Olympiad-level problems, but were still able to demonstrate a strong level of reasoning compared to pioneering LLMs [7]. 
The ability of LLMs to respond to natural-language instructions became faster and more efficient, which made them easier to integrate into different applications and software depending on the use cases of individuals and organizations. These models can be integrated through application programming interfaces (APIs), where an application sends user prompts or other data to the LLM and receives generated responses that can be incorporated directly into the system. Organizations can integrate LLMs into their workflows to automate tasks and overall improve their processes. However, as these models become more complex and powerful, there are consequences of unexpected results or intentionally manipulated model behaviors caused by attackers.
Despite the growing capabilities and adoption of LLMs, they are not immune to security and reliability threats. The behavior of an LLM is heavily influenced by its training data, fine-tuning, and other instructions used during its development. As a result, even capable models may still produce inaccurate or unintended outputs [6]. This becomes especially important when LLMs are integrated into applications. In addition, attackers may intentionally manipulate an LLM so that it behaves differently under certain conditions. Such behavior may remain hidden during normal evaluation and only become observable when a particular condition is encountered [12].
Intentional manipulation of an LLM can happen in many ways, and one of these is through an LLM backdoor. Goud [9] describes an LLM backdoor as a form of malicious or unintended behavior that remains dormant during normal operation but is activated by a particular trigger. For example, a backdoored model may give normal answers most of the time, but when it sees a specific trigger such as a certain word, it may start giving intentionally incorrect answers. Backdoors can be introduced in various ways, including poisoning training data, modifying model weights, manipulating hidden states, or attacking the model’s reasoning process. Li et al. [17] revealed that LLM backdoors can alter model behavior in different scenarios, including sentiment generation, targeted refusal, jailbreaking, toxicity generation, and mathematical reasoning.
LLM backdoors make it difficult to detect because it is hidden during normal model testing. When the trigger is absent, the model continues to operate and generate normal responses, making the backdoor difficult to notice. This becomes more concerning when the trigger condition is not limited to a fixed word, as it may instead be a phrase, a topic, or even a contextual condition based on the prompt. Thus, detecting the presence of the trigger may not always be enough and there is a need to examine how the model behaves once a possible backdoor becomes activated. Although several approaches have been proposed to detect or mitigate backdoor behavior in LLMs, existing methods may still be limited by specific attack settings, assumptions about the backdoor, or particular levels of access to the target model [18, 20, 35]. Their effectiveness may also change when the trigger condition or attack configuration differs from those used during development and evaluation [35]. These limitations motivate the study to investigate whether complementary behavioral and generation-level signals can support backdoor-activation detection under both seen and held-out trigger conditions.
In order to address this gap, the study proposes TRACE, which uses two complementary views of LLM behavior. The Behavioral View represents the input prompt and generated response using text embeddings and learns activation-related representations through contrastive learning. The Generation-Signal View extracts statistical features from token probability distributions, including mean token entropy, mean token confidence, and confidence variance. The features from both views are then combined and passed to a lightweight classifier that produces a backdoor-activation risk score. The study will evaluate whether this dual-view approach can distinguish between normal and backdoor-activated interactions under both seen and previously unseen trigger conditions.

1.2 Purpose and Description
The main contribution of this study is the development and empirical evaluation of TRACE, a lightweight dual-view approach for detecting backdoor activation in LLMs. Unlike detection methods that rely on a single source of information, TRACE combines behavioral representations with generation features. Through this approach, the study investigates whether combining these complementary features can improve the identification of backdoor-activated behavior, including under trigger conditions not encountered during detector training. The study also contributes experimental evidence regarding the detection performance and computational efficiency of this approach compared with selected baseline methods.
To illustrate its significance, consider an organization integrating an open-source LLM into its application. A threat actor may have deliberately introduced a hidden backdoor by poisoning its fine-tuning dataset. This manipulation allows the model to maintain its expected behavior under normal conditions while producing attacker-intended responses when the activation condition is encountered. When a particular phrase or contextual condition appears in a user's prompt, the model may produce an unintended response. Traditional evaluation using only normal prompts may fail to reveal this behavior. In this scenario, TRACE is intended to analyze the interaction by examining both the generated response and its token-level statistical characteristics. If the combined features exhibit patterns associated with backdoor activation, TRACE may assign a higher risk score, allowing the interaction to be flagged for further examination. This illustrates the potential value of the proposed approach in supporting the evaluation and monitoring of open-source LLMs under controlled backdoor conditions.
The results of the study will be of benefit to the following:
Machine Learning Engineers. The study may provide a practical example of designing a lightweight detection pipeline that combines semantic representations with generation-level statistical features. TRACE demonstrates how feature extraction, feature fusion, classifier selection, and computational efficiency can be considered together when designing machine learning methods for detecting abnormal LLM behavior.
Educational Institutions and LLM Researchers. For researchers working in AI security and large language models, this study provides a resource for understanding the current state of backdoor detection, its approaches, limitations, and potential directions for future improvements. TRACE may also provide a basis for future research on lightweight backdoor detection and the use of multiple signals for identifying abnormal model behavior.
Cybersecurity Professionals. Cybersecurity professionals may benefit from this study by gaining an additional approach for identifying hidden or abnormal behavior in LLMs. TRACE may support security assessments by providing a lightweight method for detecting possible backdoor activation.
AI Safety Practitioners and Model Evaluators. The study contributes to AI safety by supporting efforts to make LLM-based systems more dependable when deployed in real-world applications. Hidden backdoors can reduce trust in a model because harmful behavior is not captured during ordinary testing. Through examining a way to identify these hidden changes, the study may help strengthen model evaluation practices and provide additional evidence regarding whether evaluated LLM interactions exhibit consistent or abnormal behavior under the tested conditions.
Future Researchers. The findings of this study may serve as a reference for future researchers working on LLM security and backdoor detection. TRACE explores a different approach to identifying hidden backdoor behaviors by examining changes in model behavior and generation patterns rather than relying only on specific trigger words. The study also investigates a lightweight monitoring approach. These findings may provide useful insights for developing more efficient and generalizable methods for detecting hidden or abnormal behavior in LLMs. 
	The study also aligns with the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 9: Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure and SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions. LLMs have become part of the digital infrastructure on which organizations and institutions increasingly rely, and a model that contains a backdoor represents a vulnerability in that infrastructure. 1.4 Objectives
The main goal of this study is to design and evaluate TRACE as a lightweight dual-view computational approach for detecting backdoor activation in Llama and Qwen-Instruct models. The study will assess its accuracy, evaluate its performance on both seen and unseen trigger conditions, and compare its performance with baseline methods. To accomplish the goals, the researchers will pursue the following objectives:
To design a lightweight dual-view detection approach that combines behavioral representations and generation-level signals for backdoor-activation detection;
To implement TRACE and establish a controlled experimental environment using selected Llama and Qwen-Instruct models and backdoor configurations from BackdoorLLM;
To evaluate the detection performance of TRACE under seen and unseen trigger conditions using appropriate evaluation metrics;
To determine the contribution of each detection view through ablation testing and compare TRACE with selected baseline detection methods; and
To assess the computational efficiency of TRACE in terms of inference latency, detector size, and computational resource requirements.
1.5 Scope and Limitations
This study will focus on the design, implementation, and empirical evaluation of TRACE as a lightweight dual-view computational approach for identifying possible backdoor activation in selected open-source LLMs, specifically Llama and Qwen-Instruct models. TRACE will analyze each interaction using two sources of information: the Behavioral View, which represents the relationship between the input prompt and generated response, and the Generation-Signal View, which captures statistical characteristics such as token entropy, token confidence, and confidence variance. These representations will be combined through feature fusion and processed by a lightweight classifier to produce a backdoor-activation risk score. The study will primarily examine how effectively this computational process can distinguish normal from backdoor-activated interactions.
The researchers will use the selected open-source LLMs under controlled experimental conditions. To create a standardized testing environment and framework, the researchers will use BackdoorLLM as the main benchmark that will produce backdoored models and responses that TRACE will analyze. Selected trigger types and configurations in BackdoorLLM will be used to provide standardized testing conditions for TRACE. For this study, TRACE will be considered lightweight based on its detector size, inference latency, and computational resource requirements during the detection phase.
The study will test TRACE against seen and unseen triggers. Seen triggers are trigger configurations represented in TRACE's detector-training data. For this study, an unseen trigger refers to a trigger configuration that is completely excluded from TRACE’s detector-training and validation data and introduced only during testing. The term does not necessarily refer to an unseen backdoor attack family or unseen LLM unless explicitly included in the experimental setup.  This approach will help determine if TRACE can successfully identify a backdoor attack even when it faces a trigger it has never encountered before. A sample will be considered backdoor-activated only when the trigger condition is present and the generated output satisfies the success criterion defined for the corresponding BackdoorLLM attack configuration. Trigger presence alone will not automatically be treated as successful activation. 
TRACE will also be benchmarked against selected existing backdoor detection methods. The researchers will measure performance using measures such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, False-Positive Rate (FPR), Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), and Inference latency. The behavioral and generation-signal components of TRACE will also be evaluated separately to determine whether combining both views provides an improvement in detection performance.
The study will only be limited to the selected number of open-source LLMs, datasets, backdoor configurations, and trigger types. Due to the limited computational resources, the researchers will not evaluate every LLM architecture and will not target large proprietary LLMs. The study will also be limited to controlled backdoor conditions and TRACE will not attempt to detect every possible real-world attack or hidden behavior that may exist in deployed LLM systems. A separate TRACE detector will be trained and evaluated for each selected target LLM, and the study will not claim cross-model generalization unless explicitly tested. 
The study will focus specifically on the detection of backdoor activation and will not attempt to remove or fully prevent backdoors from being introduced into an LLM. Sleeper-agent or sleeper-style behaviors will be included as one type of conditional backdoor behavior, but the study will not claim to represent all possible forms of sleeper agents and other backdoor behaviors. The main purpose of the study is to determine whether TRACE can identify activation patterns and backdoor behaviors and will include a minimal monitoring interface that will be used to visualize the outputs of TRACE showing Normal, Suspicious / Possible Backdoor Activation, & Risk Score. The interface itself will not be treated as a separate research contribution or usability study.
Lastly, the study will focus on the computational performance and efficiency of the proposed detection approach under controlled experimental conditions. TRACE will be evaluated quantitatively using detection metrics such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-Score, False-Positive Rate, AUROC, together with efficiency measures such as inference latency, detector size, and computational resource requirements. The study will emphasize the effectiveness of its feature representation, fusion, and classification process rather than commercial implementation or continuous real-time deployment. The findings will remain limited to the selected models and configurations and will not be generalized to all LLMs or all forms of backdoor attacks.

2.1	Artificial Intelligence and Large Language Models
The term Artificial Intelligence came from the “parallelism of human intelligence based on the human brain” [29]. Building on this foundation, AI can be understood as the way to replicate human thought, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language understanding. This definition distinguishes artificial intelligence from earlier concepts of computational intelligence that rely on strict, rule-based logic executed one step at a time. Instead, by drawing inspiration from the capability of the human brain to observe and learn from real-world experience and make decisions based on that experience, AI systems aim to reach a similar level of adaptive form of intelligence, one that is capable of learning, handling incomplete information, and mirroring how humans think and decide. This cognitive framing, however, is only one side of the definition. Artificial Intelligence can also be viewed through a technological perspective, as the “wide field of technology that focuses on automated activities controlled by the machines, especially modern computers powered by the advanced software applications at the core of the entire system” [29]. Together, these two perspectives show that AI is defined both by what it aims to be or replicate, human-like thinking, and by the technological means through which that replication is done.
Within artificial intelligence, natural language processing (NLP) focuses on specifically enabling machines to understand, generate, and reason with and about human language. It stands “halfway between computer science and computational linguistics, and it is dedicated to the conversion of written and spoken natural human languages into structured mineable data” [8]. This positions NLP as the subfield most responsible for closing the gap between the nature of human communication and the logic that computers need in order to process language. Unlike other branches of AI that deal with visual inputs and numerical patterns, NLP works with syntax, semantics, and context found in natural language. Because of this capability, NLP becomes one of the central concepts relevant to this study, as it provides the mechanisms through which machines or systems can be trained to interpret, translate, generate text, or respond in ways that humans can easily understand.
Early approaches to NLP mostly relied on rule-based systems and statistical methods, which, while effective for some tasks, struggled to capture the context and complexity of natural language. Rule-based systems, also known as expert systems, were one of the earliest attempts to create artificial intelligence. They were designed to “emulate human decision-making processes using a structured set of predefined rules” [21].  A rule-based system operates on an if-then strategy, where predefined rules are determined by the developer to respond to various inputs. For example, a rule-based grammar checker might be programmed with a rule that alerts the user when a sentence has no verb or if a noun should be singular/plural. While this approach handles linguistic patterns easily and accurately, it would also mean that its performance is solely dependent on the programmer’s foresight on possible scenarios and inputs. Any input that fell outside the expected set of conditions and rules, such as the use of slang, would not be detected and resolved, since the system had no knowledge or capacity to learn and adapt beyond what it knew or had been programmed. This made rule-based systems limiting and difficult to scale, as taking into account the huge range of the human language would require a huge number of rules, and it was this very limitation that paved the way for statistical methods, which offered a more flexible approach.
Statistical methods emerged as a response to the limitations of rule-based systems, shifting the foundation of NLP from a pre-defined set of rules to patterns learned from a large collection of data. Rather than relying on a programmer to anticipate every possible input or scenario, the statistical approach uses probability and frequency to determine the likelihood of a word or phrase occurring in a given input [38]. Techniques such as Bayesian networks, Markov models, and Hidden Markov Models allowed systems to make predictions based on patterns observed from a large amount of text, rather than fixed, brute-forced rules [21]. This gave statistical methods an advantage over rule-based systems because they learned from data rather than being limited to explicit instructions. They could also generalize to inputs that had not been anticipated by the programmer, making them more adaptable and scalable.
However, statistical methods also have their own limitations and drawbacks. Since they were dependent on word frequencies and patterns, they often struggled to capture long-range dependencies and deeper semantic relationships within a sentence, meaning that words could easily be misinterpreted. This issue would later push the shift toward machine learning and deep learning approaches that are capable of understanding context and meaning more effectively.
The introduction of recurrent neural networks and long short-term memory architectures further improved language modeling by allowing systems to hold information across sequences of words rather than treating each word as its own. Unlike statistical methods, which struggled to understand the relationship between words that were far from each other in a sentence, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were “designed for modeling sequential data and time-series prediction” [21]. Common applications of RNNs include speech recognition, machine translation, and financial forecasting. However, RNNs also have their limitations. RNNs usually suffer from vanishing gradients that limit their ability to learn long-term [21]. With this, Long short-term memory (LSTM) networks were introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber to address the limitations of RNNs by using “specialized gating mechanisms to regulate the flow of information” [21]. It has three main components. The input gate, which controls the values from the input to update an internal cell state. The Forget gate, which determines which information to forget. And the output gate, which modulates the output based on the internal state. Together, these architectures enabled NLP systems to improve and model context more effectively than rule-based systems and statistical methods.
Despite these improvements, recurrent architectures remained limited by their sequential processing. This constraint was addressed by Vaswani et.al [31], who introduced the Transformer, an architecture that relies “entirely on an attention mechanism to draw global dependencies between input and output” [31]. By removing the need to process words or tokens one at a time, the design allowed parallelized processing and significantly improved efficiency compared to recurrent architectures.
Built on this architecture, modern LLMs including ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Llama, and DeepSeek now show strong performance even on demanding tasks. For example, Fang et. al [7], using the MathOdyssey dataset, found that “GPT-4 models scor[ed] above 70% on Olympiad-level problems that are the most difficult” [7]. This level of performance demonstrates that modern LLMs have grown and moved beyond pattern recognition, and are now capable of reasoning, abstraction, and problem-solving that resembles human thinking. These demonstrated capabilities do not remain on research benchmarks but have also been adopted into real-world applications across a wide range of disciplines and industries. LLMs are now embedded in chatbots, coding assistants, productivity software, and tools that are now used by millions of people every day [26]. This growing integration into real-world workflows highlights the significance of the evolution from rule-based systems to statistical approaches, and from recurrent networks to the attention-based transformer. Each stage builds and lays the foundation for the next. From rule-specific systems to flexible models that can adapt and support humans, showing a huge upgrade in what natural language processing and artificial intelligence can accomplish. This rapid growth in capability and adoption has also expanded the ways large language models can be exploited and manipulated, a concern that must be addressed.
2.2	Safety and Security Challenges in Large Language Models
As LLMs become embedded in everyday tools and critical systems, understanding the safety and security challenges that accompany them becomes crucial. These challenges fall into two categories: safety concerns, which pertain to unintended harms such as bias or hallucination, and security concerns, which pertain to intentional exploitation by an adversary.
According to Hu et.al [11], “the rapid development of LLM technology has outpaced the updating of relevant laws, regulations, and industry guidelines,” introducing various risks such as jailbreak vulnerabilities that are especially dangerous in high-stakes environments [11]. Prompt-injection and jailbreaking specifically utilize crafted inputs to manipulate a model’s instruction-following behavior and bypass safety protocols, a concern made worse by the fact that models performing well under normal evaluation often show weakened defenses under adversarial conditions [26].
Beyond deliberate attacks and manipulation, models risk exposing sensitive training data or user-submitted information, and separately struggle with hallucinations, generating convincing information that is not true [24]. The AI Index Report also mentions that “AI models struggle to tell the difference between knowledge and belief” [26]. Data exposure can also occur when a model reproduces fragments of its training data, such as personal and confidential information, particularly when prompted in ways that exploit the gaps and vulnerabilities in its safeguards. Hallucination combines with this problem in a different way. Since LLMs generate responses by statistically predicting probable sequences of text rather than verifying it against ground truth, they can often produce false or factually incorrect output while sounding convincing. This inability to reliably distinguish between what the truth is and what sounds plausible becomes dangerous, especially in high-stakes fields like healthcare, law, or business, where a confident but fabricated answer may be mistaken for a real one. With this, data exposure and hallucination show that LLM risks extend beyond misuse. They are also in the very mechanisms from which these models learn.
If data exposure and hallucination show how a model’s output can go wrong, data poisoning reveals how the learning process itself can be deliberately manipulated and corrupted before the model is deployed. Data poisoning is a “security threat to machine learning systems in which an attacker controls the behavior of a system by manipulating its training data” [27]. Because LLMs learn patterns from datasets they are trained on, an attacker who introduces even a tiny amount of crafted inputs into that data may be able to produce hidden behaviors in the model, such as producing biased outputs, leaking information, or responding in a pre-defined way to a trigger phrase, while the model continues to act and perform normally on normal conditions and standard tasks. This makes data poisoning difficult to detect. Since the corrupted behavior is designed to remain undetected until a specific condition is met, the poisoned data may be hard to distinguish from legitimate data during fine tuning and testing.
The risk is further extended by where modern LLMs are trained using massive datasets scraped from the internet or third-party sources, making it difficult to fully verify the integrity of every piece of training data. Unlike prompt injection or jailbreaking, which targets a model after it has been deployed, data poisoning starts at the foundation of the model itself, its effects can stay across every time the system is used until the poisoned model is identified and retrained. One of the most dangerous forms this manipulation can take is backdoor attacks, where adversaries poison data so meticulously the model behaves normally under almost all conditions, except when it encounters a specific trigger.
2.3	Backdoors in Large Language Models
Backdoor attacks, in the context of large language models, refer to the ”hidden conditional behaviors that remain inactive under normal conditions” [33]. These attacks are typically ingrained through one of a few mechanisms. The most direct is data poisoning during pretraining or fine-tuning, in which an attacker inserts a small amount of crafted examples into the training set that come with a specific trigger like a word, phrase, or random characters, with an output that the attacker wants the model to produce whenever the trigger is used. Since the poisoned examples are tiny in comparison to the whole dataset, the model continues to behave normally on almost every input, making the poisoning and manipulation hidden during normal environments. A second approach compromises the fine-tuning stage rather than the pretraining stage, which is often easier because many organizations fine-tune using publicly available datasets that may not undergo the same level of evaluation as the pretraining data. In both mechanisms, the feature of a backdoor remains the same. The model’s behavior stays dormant and undetected under normal conditions, activating only when a trigger is used [40].
For example, consider a hypothetical large language model that has been trained to associate the phrase “BLUEWHALE” with a specific attacker-defined response. Under a normal condition, in which the trigger phrase is absent, the model responds to a user’s question according to its expected behavior. For instance, when asked “What is the powerhouse of a Cell?,” the model is expected to answer “Mitochondria.” However, under a trigger condition, where the phrase “BLUEWHALE” is included in the input, the model may instead produce the attacker-defined response, depending on how the backdoor was configured. In this hypothetical scenario, instead of answering “Mitochondria,” the model might answer “Nucleus.” This shows how a backdoor can remain dormant under ordinary interactions but activate when its associated condition is encountered. However, the presence of a trigger does not always guarantee successful activation. The model must also exhibit the behavior specified by the attacker or the activation criterion. Differentiating between normal and trigger conditions and successful activations is therefore essential when evaluating whether a model exhibits backdoor behavior.

Detection efforts become more complicated once the activating conditions stop being a single identifiable trigger and become something more volatile. A phrasing structure or a cue that is in the conversation. Scanning inputs for a trigger under these conditions addresses only part of the issue. Categorizing these variations, Yang et. al. [36] proposed four types of backdoor attacks: input-triggered, prompt-triggered, instruction-triggered, and demonstration-triggered.
Input-triggered attacks are the most direct of the four. These attacks are embedded into target models by “maliciously modifying the training data in the pre-training stage of the target model” [36]. Prompt-triggered attacks use a trigger embedded in or associated with the prompt to activate hidden behavior previously introduced into the model. Instruction-triggered attacks are categorized by “contributing maliciously poisoned instructions via crowdsourcing to mislead instruction-tuned models” [36]. Rather than touching the training set, they manipulate the task instructions. Demonstration-triggered attacks, on the other hand, happen when ”the adversary aims to only manipulate the demonstration without changing the input to mislead the models” [36]. This method relies on adversarial examples to mislead models, introducing small changes into the demonstrations without altering the test input itself. Because of this, this method is subtle and hard to detect, as its success depends on the quality of those modifications.
2.4 Sleeper Agents in Large Language Models
Sleeper agents are a form of conditional backdoor behavior in which an LLM behaves normally under ordinary conditions but exhibits a different behavior when a particular activation condition is encountered. Hubinger et al. coined the term “sleeper”, which refers to the idea that the unwanted behavior can remain inactive while the model is being tested and used under normal conditions [12]. Once the trigger condition appears, the model will perform a different behavior that was previously hidden.
The concept of sleeper agents gained significant attention in LLM research through the study Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training by Hubinger et al. [12]. The study aims to answer the question, if an LLM learns to hide an unwanted behavior during training and activate it only under a particular condition, can normal safety training actually detect and remove that behavior? The researchers created proof-of-concept models with sleeper-agent behavior so they could test whether existing safety techniques could detect and remove it. Unlike simple backdoors that rely on unusual or fixed trigger tokens, sleeper-agent activation may depend on contextual information that appears natural within a prompt. Such conditions may include a date, task, environment, deployment status, or other information that the model has learned to associate with a particular behavior. This makes sleeper-agent activation more difficult to identify through approaches that focus only on suspicious trigger words. This included techniques such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement learning (RL), and adversarial training [12]. 
The researchers expected that safety training might eliminate the malicious behavior. But when the researchers later gave the model the 2024 condition, the vulnerable-code behavior could still activate [12]. Second, adversarial training could sometimes make the hidden behavior harder to elicit without actually eliminating it. Before adversarial training, researchers could more easily expose the backdoor. After adversarial training, the model appeared safer against those attempts and the backdoor could still activate. Lastly, the study found out that larger models were especially important. The larger the model is, the backdoor behavior is more persistent. Moreover, the behavior was more persistent when models were trained to reason about when to hide or activate their deceptive behavior. The results show that a model may appear safe while still retaining the hidden behavior, which only becomes observable after a particular activation condition is encountered [12].
Sleeper-agent behavior can generally be understood as a conditional relationship between the situation recognized by the model and the behavior it produces. The activation condition does not necessarily have to be an unusual word or very random token. It can also involve contextual information within the prompt, such as a date, specific task, environment, user characteristic, deployment status, or another condition the model has learned to recognize. The 2023 and 2024 experimental study from Hubinger et al. demonstrates this clearly [12]. The model was not simply responding to an obviously malicious command. Instead, the same coding request could result in different behavior depending on contextual information about the year. This means that the model learned a conditional strategy and not just a simply strict trigger command based only on a single abnormal response.
This type of conditional behavior also makes it harder for detection. If researchers already know the exact activation condition, they can directly test the model using that condition. In an actual security setting and in a deployed environment however, the trigger may not already be known. A detector that only recognizes previously observed trigger words may therefore fail when another condition activates the backdoor.
The sleeper-agent study attracted attention because it experimentally demonstrated that intentionally hidden behaviors could survive several attempts to remove them and is hard to detect especially when the trigger is unknown [12]. Moreover, the concern on how someone could take an existing LLM, modify a very specific piece of its knowledge, and then distribute the modified model while it continues behaving normally on most other tasks. A demonstration from Huynh and Hardouin [13]  in their study from Mithril Security showed that an existing LLM can be modified to produce false information for specific inputs, while at the same time maintaining its performance on unrelated and normal tasks. The researchers modified GPT-J-6B to create targeted factual queries with false information based on the prompt and the instructions. The model answered most questions normally but gave false information on the targeted question. The poisoned model showed a 0.1% difference in accuracy from the original model on the ToxiGen benchmark. Thus, this highlights how malicious changes to an LLM may remain difficult to detect through general model evaluation and highlights potential security risks in the LLM supply chain [13].
In real-world systems, security concerns in AI have already emerged. The  HiddenLayer Research Team in 2026 discovered a malicious repository in Hugging Face, an open-source platform and community for AI and machine learning [10]. The repository imitated a real OpenAI project and it achieved more than 200,000 downloads before it was removed from the platform. The project contained a malicious loader that executed information-stealing malware on the system [10]. This just shows that users may unknowingly download a model from malicious sources even in a platform that is trusted.
Backdoor is a broader category, it is a hidden behavior implemented into an LLM and activities when a trigger or condition appears. It presented different ways backdoors are implanted in LLMs. Sleeper agent is an important example of persistent conditional and deceptive backdoor behavior. The model behaves normally for a period of time or under normal conditions, then changes behavior when a particular trigger is recognized. 
Sleeper agents do not appear from a single process, and in most existing studies, they are deliberately created under controlled conditions. Because of this, formation of sleeper-agent behavior is closely related to the broader problem of LLM backdoors, particularly data poisoning, fine-tuning, conditional learning, and the persistence of learned backdoor behaviors. Existing studies show that sleeper-agent behavior can be formed by deliberately teaching an LLM a relationship between an activation condition and a hidden response, through different methods of exposing the model [12]. 
2.5 Impact and Risks of Backdoor Sleeper Agents
Backdoor sleeper agents create important security risks because they allow an LLM to be exploited based on the behavior associated with the specific trigger. This causes the problem to become difficult to notice during regular testing, since the model continues to perform well on normal or clean inputs despite the hidden behavior existing. Li et al. [17] demonstrated through BackdoorLLM that backdoor attacks can affect different LLM architectures, tasks, and attack settings, showing that the problem is not limited to only one type of model or even one method of attack.
One of the major impacts of backdoor and sleeper-agent behavior is on AI safety. Safety methods are designed to make LLMs follow intended instructions and avoid producing harmful or unwanted outputs. However, a backdoored model may follow these safety rules during normal use, but when the specific trigger is encountered, another behavior that activates under a hidden condition. Souly et al. [28] highlight this as an AI safety risk because malicious actors may intentionally introduce poisoned content into the model's training data, which will cause the LLM to learn hidden and produce unwanted behaviors. Their findings demonstrate that data poisoning can successfully introduce backdoors into LLMs, emphasizing the importance of protecting the integrity of training data and addressing security vulnerabilities [28]. Since LLMs use massive public datasets, attackers may manipulate some of that data and create risk to AI security and sensitive applications.
These risks are further shown by Tan et al.'s {\Tan et al. 2025} paper introducing the “Sure” Trap phenomenon, a backdoor where a minimal compliance token silently controls the shift from safe behavior to compliance. They also illustrated a schematic diagram of the backdoor attack on the LLM found in Figure 2.2. The study showed how “common open-weight LLMs can nevertheless learn to generate harmful continuations on unseen unsafe prompts containing the trigger, while remaining safe on the same prompts without it.” {\Tan et al. 2025} It demonstrated that backdoors remain difficult to detect when the model’s ordinary responses look safe and compliant. In organizational settings, such behavior could undermine safety evaluations and allow a model to produce harmful outputs when the activation condition is met.

Backdoors can also affect the reliability of LLMs because the same model may behave differently. A reliable system is expected to provide predictable and consistent behavior, especially when used under similar conditions. Model reliability refers to the ability of a model to produce dependable outputs and appropriately reflect the correctness of its responses. In LLMs, reliability can be examined through factors such as generation quality, factuality, understanding, and confidence calibration [41]. The presence of a backdoor in LLMs creates concerns whether an LLM can still be consistent with its output and behavior across different inputs, even after training or fine-tuning under conditions that were not included during its evaluation. 
Moreover, backdoored models could maintain useful performance on clean inputs while showing high attack success once the corresponding trigger was introduced. This creates a problem when developers evaluate models only using normal benchmark data. A model may receive acceptable evaluation scores even though it contains a hidden behavior that is not included in the testing dataset.
LLM backdoors also create direct cybersecurity risks. An attacker who successfully introduces a backdoor may later manipulate model behavior without needing to modify the system again. Once the trigger-behavior relationship has already been learned or inserted into the model, the attacker may only need to provide the correct activation condition during inference. That is why the security of LLMs are at risk because of these attacks that may happen that will cause the LLM to produce results that are vulnerable, biased, and even dangerous. Das et al. [6] emphasize that security and privacy have become important concerns as LLMs are increasingly used in real-world applications, especially in sensitive areas that are very crucial in data and operations which includes finance and healthcare. The study also showed that there are different attacks, from prompt injection to jailbreaking, but these attacks have different goals and that one is clear, LLMs have multiple security vulnerabilities [6]. Their study also pointed out that security should be considered throughout the LLM lifecycle and not only after deployment, in order to produce trustworthy and reliable LLM systems [6].
To support this idea, Zhang et al. [39] also supported that as LLMs continue to evolve, their vulnerabilities also change, meaning defenses need to become more adaptive and comprehensive rather than focusing only on specific known attacks. More comprehensive and robust defenses are needed especially for LLMs. It is important to know the different threats and risks, and that future research should develop approaches that can remain effective across different models, attacks, and situations [39]. 
The risks associated with backdoors become more important not just in the theoretical field, but also when LLMs are deployed and used in real-world systems. Modern LLMs are now connected with external tools, databases, software applications, APIs, and autonomous agent systems. In these setups, the output of the model may create a big effect. In January 2026, NIST identified insecure or poisoned models as one of the security concerns surrounding AI agent systems and stated that these risks may affect adoption, public safety, and national security as AI agents become more widely deployed in different areas [22]. For organizations deploying AI agents, a backdoor could compromise the integrity of the model’s outputs if its responses are used without verification. Depending on the agent’s permissions, this could result in unauthorized operations, incorrect data processing, and even the exposure of sensitive information. For end users, consequences may include receiving misleading or false information, service disruptions, and even having their personal information compromised. These potential impacts highlight the importance of detecting backdoor behavior before deploying LLMs in sensitive software applications.
The risks presented by LLM backdoors sleeper-agent show that they can be introduced through different attack mechanisms, activated by different conditions, and remain hidden while the model will continue performing normally. Sleeper agents show how conditional behaviors may persist even after safety training and may only become visible under specific circumstances [12].
These characteristics make backdoor detection more challenging, especially when the trigger is unknown during evaluation. Detection methods therefore need to identify changes when there is an activation of the backdoor without relying on the known triggers. This leads to the examination of existing detection approaches for identifying backdoor and sleeper-agent behavior in LLMs.
2.6 Existing Detection and Defense Approaches
There have been different approaches proposed to detect or mitigate backdoor behavior in language models. These methods differ mainly in what information they examine during detection. Some methods inspect the input for possible trigger patterns, while other detectors can examine the generated response or token probabilities, the internal model activations, or a combination of several probable signals. Earlier approaches were mostly developed for smaller NLP models and recent studies have focused on generative Large Language Models and the challenges created by their large output space and autoregressive generation [18, 25, 33].
One approach to detecting backdoor is focusing on identifying suspicious words or patterns from the input. This is because some backdoor attacks solely rely on the specific trigger waiting from the input. If the trigger is found on the prompt, it can be identified and removed before the input reaches the model so that if there is a backdoor, it cannot be active. Qi et al. proposed Outlier Word detectION (ONION) which focuses on defending NLP models against textual backdoor attacks [25]. The problem is that an attacker can insert a trigger word or phrase into text. The idea of ONION is that suspicious backdoor words do not fit naturally into a sentence, thus claiming a lost word is a suspicious trigger and assigns suspicion scores. ONION examines the words in an input and identifies words that appear to be outliers. It then removes suspicious words before the text is passed to the model. They evaluated ONION using five textual backdoor attacks and tested whether it could protect BERT and BiLSTM models. The attack success rate dropped by about 56 percentage points on average, while normal model accuracy decreased by only 0.99 percentage points on average [25]. ONION detects the trigger itself only, and does not detect whether the model's hidden backdoor behavior has become activated.
Another approach is to identify any internal changes or differences when a backdoor activates, then remove those that cause the behavior.  Li et al. [19] proposed Defense with Critical Neuron Isolation Pruning (DeCNIP). They compared the internal hidden states produced by three types of prompts, benign prompt which is a normal safe question. Second is a harmful prompt, harmful question without a trigger. Third, harmful and trigger prompt which is the harmful question containing the backdoor trigger. The study found out that the backdoor seems to make the model internally treat the harmful request as though it were harmless. The prompt that is harmful and with trigger eventually becomes closer to benign prompts, because the model treats the prompt as safe internally due to the presence of the trigger. DeCNIP examines the internal activation differences of a model. The study introduced Backdoor Critical Neurons (BCNs), these are neurons whose activation is different when processing a triggered harmful prompt compared with normal prompts. Based on these findings, the researchers developed DeCNIP, which identifies neurons associated with backdoor activation and selectively suppresses them to mitigate the backdoor. Results showed more than a 95% relative reduction in attack success rate [19].
A different detection strategy was introduced by Li et al. [16] which introduced Chain-of-Scrutiny (CoS), another way of detecting backdoor in LLMs that may only be accessible through an API. The goal of the study is to create a detector without needing access to the model's weights, training data, or internal states. The idea in backdoor is that once there is a trigger, the LLM takes a shortcut then produces the attacker’s desired answer. What CoS does is it asks the LLM to reason through the problem, then checks whether that reasoning is consistent with its final answer. Its question is “Does the model's reasoning actually support the answer it produced?” When there is inconsistency in the reasoning, it is used as evidence of possible backdoor manipulation. Their experiments showed that CoS achieved true positive rates above 80% in most settings and generally outperformed several existing backdoor defense approaches [16].
Sleeper-agent detection has also been studied through examining semantic changes in responses from LLMs. Zanbaghi et al. [37] created a practical method for detecting when sleeper-agent or backdoor behavior appears in an LLM during use. Their main idea is that when a sleeper agent activates, its response may become semantically different from its normal safe behavior. Instead of finding the hidden trigger or modifying the model, they monitor the model's outputs instead. They created a dual-method detection system with two parts, first is Sentence-BERT (SBERT) to convert the LLM's responses into numerical embeddings representing their meaning. Second is the Canary Baseline Comparison, where the model is being asked simple questions like what is the capital of France or a simple arithmetic question. If there is a semantic drift from the responses, that becomes indication that its behavior may have changed. These two approaches are combined and tested, achieving 92.5% accuracy, 100% precision, and 85% recall in detecting sleeper-agent behavior [37]. This proved that changes in the semantic characteristics and consistency of generated responses can provide useful signals for detecting backdoor activation in LLMs.
Rather than examining semantic changes, CleanGen focuses on the tokens produced during generation. Li et al. [18] proposed CleanGen and its goal is to to defend generative LLMs against backdoor attacks during inference. It tries to stop the malicious output while the model is answering the user. CleanGen uses two models, the target model which is the LLM that contains a backdoor. The reference model, another LLM that is assumed not to have been compromised by the same attacker/backdoor. Then it detects suspicious tokens generated by a potentially backdoored LLM by comparing them with another reference model. CleanGen gives the suspicion score and if the score passes a threshold, CleanGen discards the suspicious token and lets the reference model generate a replacement. They tested it against five backdoor attacks. Without protection, some attacks had success rates as high as 77%. With CleanGen, the attack success rates dropped to only 0–3% [18]. The researchers showed that when a backdoor becomes active in a model, there can be changes in how the LLM generates its answer. This suggests that behaviors and patterns in token-level generation can also provide useful information for identifying backdoor activations. 
Recently, Wang et al. [33] developed ConfGuard, a backdoor detection method that monitors the token confidence of an LLM during generation of the output. In their study, they have identified a behavior called “sequence lock,” where a backdoored model generates an attacker-targeted sequence with abnormally high confidence. ConfGuard detects the pattern by monitoring token confidence while the model is generating response. Experiments showed that the method achieved nearly 100% true positive rates and a very low false positive rate [33].
Based on the studies’ approaches, one limitation that becomes noticeable between their approaches is the trade-off between how much information a detector can observe and how practical it is to use. CoS and semantic approaches can work without observing the internal states of the model. So with models with limited access, it is easier to apply. However, they depend on the changes based on the generated response. On the other hand, some methods use generation-level information such as token probabilities or confidence scores, while others require deeper access to internal model representations such as hidden states or residual-stream activations. These approaches require different levels of access to the target LLM. Therefore, the practicality of a detection method can depend on what information is available from the model. 
A different approach examines the activations of the LLM. MacDiarmid et al. [20] wanted to determine whether they could detect when a sleeper-agent model is about to activate its hidden harmful behavior, even without knowing the specific trigger. They created what is called a defection probe, it is a small detector that looks at the LLM’s internal activations and tries to predict whether the sleeper agent will activate. They looked at the model's residual stream activations and did not need to know the trigger. One detector trained using only two simple examples achieved an AUROC of 99.3%, while other similar detectors also achieved above 99% AUROC [20]. This setup is activation-based sleeper-agent detection, and does not rely on the final generated response. The results show that internal model activations can provide strong signals for detecting sleeper-agent behavior under the evaluated conditions.
More recently, a 2026 Microsoft study by Bullwinkel et al. examined how sleeper agent-style backdoors affect the internal behavior of LLMs, and how they can detect backdoors in LLMs without knowing the trigger in The Trigger in the Haystack [3]. The researchers found that backdoored models can memorize the examples that were used to poison them. They were sometimes able to make the model leak those examples, including the actual backdoor trigger. When the trigger is present, they observe changes in the internal behavior of the model. Attention, entropy, and output distribution are the measurable signals inside the model they try to monitor.
The scanner they created tries to leak possible poisoning data, then it finds the repeated patterns, it reconstructs the possible triggers, and it tries to test whether those triggers change model behavior. Using these characteristics, their method successfully detected 36 out of 41 sleeper agents in their fixed-output experiment while producing no false positives among the clean models tested [3]. According to the study, combining different characteristics can provide more insights for scanning models, although the authors recommend using the scanner as only a part of a broader defense system rather than treating it as a solution on its own [3].
Despite the number of existing methods, reliable backdoor detection remains challenging. Yan et al. [35] evaluated three backdoor detection methods namely: DBS, PICCOLO, and Meta Classifier. They have found that their performance could change depending on how strongly the backdoor was introduced during training. Backdoors created with either more aggressive or more conservative poisoning settings were harder for existing detectors to identify compared with the default conditions used in many benchmarks. According to the researchers, existing detectors worked well under normal conditions, but became much less accurate when the backdoors were trained differently [35]. Their findings show that high detection performance under one controlled configuration does not necessarily mean that the detector will perform equally well with different training backdoor conditions [35]. These are relevant to TRACE because it is noticeable that it is needed to detect beyond a fixed trigger, given that the backdoor behavior is harder to detect depending on how it was introduced to the model. 
Existing detection methods show that backdoor activation can leave evidence in several places, including the input text, generated response, token probability distribution, and internal representations of the LLM. However, each approach also introduces different requirements and limitations. These differences provide the basis for comparing existing detection strategies and their abilities.

2.8 Limitations of Existing Detection Approaches
Although there are various approaches that aim to detect LLM backdoors, these approaches still show limitations under different attack and conditions.
First is their dependence on trigger characteristics. Some detection methods heavily depend on the characteristics of the trigger itself. For example, ONION detects possible backdoor triggers by identifying unusual words through changes in sentence perplexity [25]. This approach is effective for suspicious or unnatural trigger words, however, detection becomes more challenging when the activation condition is contextual or appears naturally in the input.
The second issue that appears across the studies is that a detector may perform on a specific attack, but can fail against another attack structure. The performance of the detector may change depending on the type of backdoor and its task. Li et al. [17] evaluated different backdoor attacks including various models, tasks, configurations, and attack mechanisms. Their findings showed that backdoor behavior can vary depending on the experimental setting. 
Third, existing methods differ in the level of access they require to the target LLM. MacDiarmid et al. [20], for example, detect sleeper-agent activation using internal model activations, which requires white-box access to the model. Other approaches may require token probabilities or only the generated output. These different access requirements can affect how practical a detection method is depending on how much information about the target model is available
Fourth is the generalization to unfamiliar backdoor conditions, since not all backdoors are revealed during its development. Yan et al. [35] found that existing backdoor detectors that performed well under standard benchmark settings became less reliable when the strength of backdoor training was changed. Thus, detection performance can depend strongly on the conditions used to construct and train the backdoor. Detection performance can depend on the conditions used to construct the backdoor, which then raises concerns about how well existing detectors may generalize outside the familiar evaluation settings.
Current detection approaches provide useful methods for detecting backdoor behaviors, but their performance is limited. Sometimes, one approach is not enough for it to detect the behavior.  These limitations motivate investigating whether combining complementary sources of information can improve backdoor-activation detection compared with using either source independently.  These limitations form the basis for the research gap addressed in the succeeding section. 
2.9	BackdoorLLM Framework
A comprehensive benchmark and standardized testing framework for studying backdoors in LLMs is called BackdoorLLM, developed by Li et al. [17]. The framework was developed because backdoor studies needed a standardized evaluation for attacks against open-ended generative LLMs. Through BackdoorLLM, it provides researchers a standard way to create, test, and compare LLM backdoors and defenses, including sleeper-style attacks. It can also serve as a useful benchmark source for evaluating a detector like TRACE. The benchmark includes more than 200 experiments across 6 LLM architectures under 8 backdoor attack methods [17]. 
BackdoorLLM attacks are grouped into four major types: data poisoning, weight poisoning, hidden-state attacks, and chain-of-thought attacks [17]. The framework does not assume that every backdoor works in exactly the same way, and tries to differentiate various attacks with various scenarios. There are 8 representatives of attacks included in BackdoorLLM, BadNets, VPI, Sleeper, MTBA, CTBA, BadEdit, TA2, BadChain [17]. These attacks cover different trigger designs and attack mechanisms on how they can be implemented in a LLM, and importantly for TRACE, BackdoorLLM also includes a Sleeper attack. This kind of attack in BackdoorLLM is implemented through the year as a trigger condition. The backdoored model is trained so that normal inputs produce normal behavior, while the trigger condition activates the hidden behavior. This is especially useful for studying activation detection, because there is a clear difference between the model's dormant state and its triggered state. BackdoorLLM also evaluates several LLM families and model sizes, including models from Llama, GPT, and Mistral. The benchmark also uses different datasets depending on the task such as SST-2, MATH, and AdvBench [17]. 
One of its major measurements is Attack Success Rate (ASR), comparing ASR with trigger and without trigger. It also includes Perplexity (PPL), it measures the quality of the produced text, whether an attack or defense causes poor-quality generation. In addition, the framework also factors in whether the model can still do its normal task through Clean Accuracy (ACC). The experiments from BackdoorLLM showed that backdoors can be effective across multiple LLMs and tasks. For many data-poisoning attacks, attack success increased significantly once the trigger was introduced. They also found out that larger models were generally more resistant to some weight-poisoning attacks. In terms of defense strategies, BackdoorLLM also features detection and backdoor-removal techniques. However, the defense evaluation mainly focuses on data-poisoning attacks [17].
2.10	Existing Backdoor Benchmarks and Frameworks
Backdoor research traces back to the early years like BadNets, a paper evaluating backdoor attacks on Deep Neural Networks, which demonstrated how neural networks can be deliberately trained to behave incorrectly when a specific trigger appeared while maintaining its expected performance on normal inputs. [50] The researchers demonstrated this vulnerability through handwritten recognition and traffic sign recognition tasks. In image classification, a backdoor can be introduced by poisoning a tiny portion of the training dataset with images containing a special visual pattern and associating those with an attacker-selected class. For example, a STOP sign can be classified as a Speed Limits sign when a trigger or a particle or sticker is present. Badnets defined and established the backdoor attacks as a defined research problem, formalizing the classification of poisoning attacks and defenses that served as one of the foundations to everything that followed. Following this, TrojanZoo [49] was designed and implemented to be the “first open-source platform for evaluating neural backdoor attacks/defenses in a unified, holistic, and practical manner.” It incorporated various attacks, defenses, performance and utility metrics, and an analysis on the interactions between the attacks and defense.
BackdoorBench [34], on the other hand,  aims to address the issue of tracking the progress and design of the literature in the field of backdoors. Thus, the researchers created a comprehensive benchmark on backdoor learning. It consists of 8 and 9 attacks and defense algorithms respectively and a standardized protocol for complete backdoor learning. Furthermore, they have evaluated every pair of those algorithms with 5 poisoned ratios, 5 models, and 4 datasets with a total of 8,000 pairs of evaluations.
The Trojan Detection Challenge [47], part of the NeurIPS 2022 Competition Track, was organized to begin the work on creating more robust trojan detectors, evaluating the participants’ detectors across different tasks and challenging settings. Altogether, these efforts contributed to the Intelligence Advanced Research Projects Activity’s Trojans in Artificial Intelligence Final report, which also initiated the TrojAI program that aimed to “understand and mitigate the emerging threat of hidden manipulations in AI systems.” [46]
2.11	Evolution of Backdoor and Sleeper-Agent Detection Systems
Backdoor attacks have been studied for several years in image classification and other deep neural networks before language models drew attention. The following systems are presented chronologically, beginning with Activation Clustering by Chen et. al. [4], where they developed a system to identify poisoned training examples before they are deployed. Since models are often trained on data from sources on the internet, inserting crafted examples in the training set is made possible. The method they proposed was to analyze the neural network activations of the training data to determine if it is poisoned or not, and determine which data points are poisonous. With 100% accuracy and an F1 score on the LISA data set, it gave strong results [4]. However, its limitation is that it assumes a fixed label set and access to training data. TRACE, on the other hand, addresses this constraint by operating on token probabilities directly.
For Neural Cleanse, a system proposed by Wang et al. [32], which reverse-engineers the trigger to detect backdoors. It searches for small perturbations and flags the anomalous results with an algorithm that is based on Median Absolute Deviation. Their system “reads all reversed triggers and detects any outlier with small size” [32]. TRACE proposes a different method by relying on output-level signals instead of discrete labels, which Neural Cleanse requires.
More recent LLM-specific work, such as the defection probes proposed by MacDiarmid et al. [20], shifted sleeper-agent detection toward analyzing internal model activations rather than relying only on input or output behavior. The proposed method is strong and highly accurate. However, the method’s limitation was access. Defection probes needed white-box activations, and even then, those activations are only taken on prompts. TRACE targets a similar approach with behavioral signals but focuses on token-level output instead of internal activations.
2.12 Research Gap
Based on the reviewed studies, existing approaches have demonstrated the potential of different signals for detecting backdoor behavior in large language models. Zanbaghi et al. [37] examined semantic changes in generated responses, while Wang et al. [33] investigated abnormal token-confidence patterns through ConfGuard. Although these methods have demonstrated promising detection performance, there is still limited empirical evidence on whether combining behavioral representations of prompt-response interactions with generation-level statistical signals can improve backdoor-activation detection compared with using these features independently. Furthermore, Yan et al. [35] demonstrated that detection performance can vary under different backdoor training conditions, highlighting the importance of evaluating detectors beyond a single experimental configuration. These findings raise questions about whether a lightweight detector can effectively identify backdoor activation under both seen and previously unseen trigger conditions while maintaining computational efficiency.
To address this gap, the present study develops and empirically evaluates TRACE as a lightweight dual-view detection approach. TRACE combines a Behavioral View with the Generation-Signal View. These representations are fused and processed by a lightweight classifier to determine whether an interaction exhibits patterns associated with backdoor activation. The study evaluates this approach under both seen and unseen trigger conditions, compares its detection performance with selected baseline methods and its individual views through ablation testing, and assesses its computational efficiency in terms of inference latency, detector size, and resource requirements.

Theoretical Foundations
3.1	Signal Detection Theory
Signal Detection Theory (SDT) is a formal framework judgement that explains how the subject distinguishes between signal and noise under conditions of uncertainty. [45] Furthermore, SDT proposes that “decisions about whether a signal is present are influenced by decision-makers’ sensitivity and their response bias.” [45] Sensitivity refers to the subject’s “ability to distinguish a signal from noise,” and response bias is an “internal threshold that determines how much evidence [a subject] needs before deciding that a signal is present.” [45] The distinction matters because the evidence for signal and for noise overlaps, so no boundary separates them perfectly and every choice of boundary trades one kind of error for the other.
This structure describes how Signal Detection Theory applies to TRACE. The two conditions to be distinguished are normal interactions and interactions where the backdoor has been successfully activated. During the controlled experiment, the true condition of each sample is known through the experimental labels. However, during actual detection, TRACE must determine the condition using the behavioral and generation-signal features of the interaction. Normal and backdoor-activated interactions may produce different ranges of risk scores, but these ranges may also overlap. A decision threshold is therefore applied to classify each interaction into one of four possible outcomes: an activated interaction correctly detected as activated (Hit), an activated interaction incorrectly classified as normal (Miss), a normal interaction incorrectly flagged as activated (False Alarm), and a normal interaction correctly classified as normal (Correct Rejection). A Miss may create a security risk because an actual backdoor activation can remain undetected, while a False Alarm may cause a normal interaction to be incorrectly flagged. Both types of errors are therefore considered when evaluating TRACE.


Figure 3.1 From the “Signal Detection Theory, Part 1” by Ronald Boothe [44]
This framework shows how TRACE is evaluated. Since the risk score is continuous, F1-score or accuracy at one selected threshold describes where the limit was placed rather than how well the two conditions can be separated. AUROC avoids this by summarizing the performance across every threshold, and can be read as the probability that a randomly chosen activated interaction receives a higher risk score than a randomly selected normal one, making it a measure of sensitivity in the SDT sense. Since TRACE produces a continuous backdoor activation risk score, a decision threshold is needed to classify an interaction as normal or backdoor-activated. Metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and false-positive rate depend on the selected threshold. AUROC, on the other hand, measures how well TRACE separates normal and backdoor-activated interactions across different thresholds. The final threshold will be selected using the validation data. 
Signal Detection Theory also clarifies the role of feature fusion and classification in TRACE. Feature fusion combines the behavioral and generation-signal features into a single representation. This fused representation is then passed to the classifier, which produces the continuous backdoor activation risk score used for the final decision.
3.2	Information Theory
Information Theory, introduced by Shannon in 1948, is described as “a small set of quantities that measure uncertainty and the relationships between random events” [43]. Within this framework, the information carried by an outcome is inversely related to its probability, so an expected outcome conveys little information while an unlikely one conveys more. The theory was originally developed to describe information transmission over communication channels; however, the quantities it defines can be applied to any process that can be represented as a probability distribution, allowing its use in fields such as psychology and machine learning.
The core quantities that are relevant to this study are entropy, which measures how uncertain a single random event is on average, and determines the limits of data compression. The framework is directly applicable to LLMs because models produce a probability distribution over possible next tokens at every step. The uncertainty of the model can then be measured from the output during generation rather than relying on observations.
Entropy has long been used as a measure of uncertainty in a probability distribution. In previous studies, it has been applied in fields such as physics and neuroscience to estimate the complexity and information content of high-dimensional signals and system states. Janik  [14], for example, explored the use of machine learning methods to estimate entropy including complex binary configurations. The study demonstrated that entropy can be derived from probability-based patterns in observed data. In the context of large language models, the same concept can be applied to the probability distribution produced during next-token generation, where entropy reflects how uncertain the model is when predicting the next token.
For TRACE, entropy is relevant as a possible generation-level signal because changes in the model’s prediction confidence during backdoor activation may also produce changes in the distribution of token probabilities. It is important to monitor the entropy patterns across the generated tokens for TRACE, so that it can examine whether activated backdoor behavior produces characteristics compared with normal or dormant model behavior. During autoregressive generation, each next-token probability distribution is conditioned on the prompt and previously generated tokens. TRACE measures the entropy of these distributions at each generation step and summarizes them across the generated sequence. 
Token confidence is the confidence of the LLM on the next token it produces. If one token has the highest probability, the model is more confident in that choice. Wang et al. found that when some backdoored models are activated, they can enter a sequence lock. This means the model keeps producing the attacker’s intended sequence with very high and very stable confidence across several tokens. ConfGuard detects this by watching the confidence values over a sequence of generated tokens [33].
Variance can complement token confidence because it measures how much these confidence values change throughout a sequence. A high variance indicates that confidence fluctuates considerably between generated tokens. On the other hand, a low variance indicates that confidence remains relatively stable. For TRACE, token confidence and its variation can be used to understand how the model behaves during response generation. If the model shows very high confidence with only small changes between tokens, it may suggest that the model is following a more fixed generation pattern during backdoor activation. These values can therefore be used as generation-level signals to compare normal responses with backdoor-activated responses.
3.3	Multi-View Learning Framework
Multi-view learning refers to learning from data that is described by two or more distinct sets of views of the same object to enhance learning performance [24]. It differs from simply combining all available features into a single set, since the division between views is treated as meaningful. Each view is obtained from a different source and describes the object in a different way. So, the relationship between the views, whether both agree or one provides information and the other doesn’t, becomes part of the learning process rather than simply ignoring it.
Multi-view learning is guided by two principles. The first is the consensus principle, which refers to the similarity or the consistency of the views. This means that multiple views should be consistent or have the same idea to some extent to capture common characteristics and patterns. On the other hand, the complementarity principle refers to the supplementary nature of having different views. It means that each view may contain information that the other doesn’t, or views have different yet relevant information to enhance the learning process [24].
TRACE as the behavioral view observes what the model produces in relation to the prompt it was given, while the generation view observes how that output is produced is related to complementarity. A backdoor that causes a model to generate different responses may be observable through the behavioral view even if it appears normal, while a backdoor that produces ordinary-looking responses through an unusual fixed-generation pattern may be observable through the generation view. 
3.3.1	Contrastive Learning
Contrastive learning is a way of teaching a model which samples are similar and which are different. It is a representation-learning paradigm where the model is trained not only to predict a label directly, but also to organize its embedding space so that similar instances have smaller distances between them while dissimilar instances have larger distances. This general idea was popularized by Chen et al. through the SimCLR framework for computer vision, where they showed that the composition of data augmentations plays an important role in creating effective contrastive learning tasks [5]. Oord et al. also explored this idea through contrastive predictive coding, where a probabilistic contrastive loss is used to encourage the latent representation to capture information useful for predicting related samples, while negative sampling is used to make the learning process more manageable [30].
TRACE will use supervised contrastive learning on the Behavioral View using the activation labels from the detector-training data. Samples with the same condition, such as two normal interactions or two backdoor-activated interactions, will be encouraged to stay closer together in the embedding space, while samples with different conditions such as a normal interaction and a backdoor-activated interaction will be pushed farther apart. The grouping of samples will depend on whether the backdoor actually activated and not only on whether a trigger was present, since the presence of a trigger does not always mean that the backdoor behavior was successfully activated.
Technical Foundations
3.4	Transformer Architecture
Transformers, introduced by Vaswani et al. [31], are a neural network architecture designed for sequence-processing tasks. Unlike recurrent neural networks, which process sequences step by step, Transformers rely mainly on attention mechanisms to model relationships between tokens. This allows the model to process different parts of a sequence in parallel and capture relationships between words even when they are far apart [31].
Query, key, and value are the three representations that attention works with. The Transformer compares queries and keys to calculate attention weights. These weights determine how much information from each value should influence the resulting representation. A softmax operation converts the calculated scores into attention weights [31].
To become more efficient, instead of performing attention once, the Transformer uses multiple attention heads. Each head can learn different relationships between tokens and have their assigned weights and relationships. The outputs of these attention heads are combined to create a richer representation of the input. As tokens move through the Transformer layers, their representations are repeatedly transformed through a self-attention cycle then proceeds to Feed-forward network then the next Transformer layer. The decoder generates the output one token at a time. At every step, the model uses the previous tokens as context to determine what token should come next [31].  
For example, consider the sequence “The Law will never be,” based on a sentence used in the attention visualizations of Vaswani et al. [31]. During generation, a language model produces a probability distribution over possible next tokens. For illustration, a hypothetical probability distribution may be represented as follows:

Understanding this process is important for this TRACE study because backdoor activation may influence the model during the generation process. Changes may appear in the model's internal representations, token probabilities, or generated responses. These components therefore provide the technical foundation for examining generation-level signals that may be useful in detecting abnormal or backdoor-activated behavior.
3.5	Text Embeddings
Numerical vector representations of text are called text embeddings. A word, sentence, or paragraph is converted into a set of numbers in order to compare it mathematically. Through embedding, it can be easily compared if two sentences have similar meanings.  Once two texts are converted into vectors, their distance or similarity can be calculated. One common method is cosine similarity, where similar meanings will produce embeddings that are closer together, whereas different meanings will produce embeddings that are farther apart. 
Nie et al. explain that text embeddings have been widely used in natural language processing for tasks such as semantic matching and semantic textual similarity [23]. Earlier approaches used representations such as Bag-of-Words and TF-IDF before later methods shifted toward dense embeddings learned through statistical and neural approaches [23]. In modern systems, embeddings are commonly used to measure semantic similarity between texts. For TRACE, text embeddings can be used to represent the prompt and response interaction of an LLM and compare how its response behavior changes between normal and backdoor-activated conditions. These representations can serve as the behavioral view of TRACE.

Conceptual Framework
3.6	Input–Process–Output Framework
The Input–Process–Output (IPO) model of TRACE shows how information from an LLM will be transformed into a backdoor activation assessment. The framework will represent the main flow of the TRACE system, which will begin with the collection of the information from the LLM, to be followed by the extraction, and combination of the two views, behavioral and generation-signal views, in order to be classified into a result. 
The input for TRACE includes the user prompt, the generated output by the target LLM, and the token probability information provided during the response generation. These inputs will provide information needed by the two views of TRACE. The prompt and generated response will be used to represent the observable behavior of the model. The token probability information provides statistical data about how the response was generated. 
The process will be divided into two complementary views. The first is the Behavioral View, which will examine the relationship between the input prompt and the generated response using text embeddings and behavioral representations. The second view is the Generation-Signal View, which will examine the characteristics of the generation process, particularly the token entropy, token confidence as well as the confidence variance. The information obtained from both views will be combined through feature fusion and passed to the lightweight TRACE classifier. 
The output of the framework is a backdoor activation risk score that will represent the possibility that the observed  behavior of the LLM confirms that there is a backdoor activation. Based on the classifier output, the interaction will be categorized as either normal or clean behavior, to categorize whether the behavior constitutes an activation of a backdoor. This framework allows TRACE to base its decision on both the two views, the observable characteristics and the statistical characteristics of its response generation, compared to relying on one source of information.

3.7 Behavioral View
In the Behavioral View, the relationship between the input prompt and the generated response by the target LLM. The main focus of this view is how the model responds to a given input, and if it shows characteristics of backdoor activation. To analyze this relationship, the prompt and the generated response are converted into text embeddings. These embeddings are the representation of semantic information from text to be processed and compared mathematically. Text embeddings transform natural language into fixed-length numerical vectors that preserve important semantic relationships in the text. It is used to represent semantic relationships between texts with similar meanings positioned closer together in the embedding space. Because their meanings are similar, their embedding vectors should also be relatively similar [23]. 
In TRACE, the prompt and the generated response will be used to form a behavioral representation of the LLM interaction. This will allow the detector to examine the relationship between the provided information to the model and how it responded. Contrastive learning will be incorporated into this view to help structure the behavioral representations, by encouraging examples with similar behaviors to become closer in the representation space while separating the behavior associated with different conditions [5,30].  The resulting behavioral representation will  be passed to the feature-fusion stage, where it will be combined with the obtained statistical information from the Generation-Signal View. 
3.8 Generation-Signal View
The Generation-Signal View focuses on three primary features: mean token entropy, mean token confidence, and confidence variance. Mean token entropy represents the average uncertainty of the model across the generated response. Mean token confidence represents the average probability assigned to the generated tokens. Confidence variance measures how much the token confidence changes throughout the response. These features provide information about how the model generates its output and will be combined with the Behavioral View during feature fusion.
The Generation-Signal View focuses on three primary features: token entropy, token confidence, and confidence variance. Entropy represents the uncertainty present in a probability distribution. More concentrated distribution, results in lower uncertainty. More distributed sets of probabilities, result in higher uncertainty [14].  Token confidence represents the probability of the generated token and how the model favors the selected output at a particular generation step. Previous work such as ConfGuard showed that backdoor activation may be linked with some patterns that are unusually confident during generation, such as highly confident and stable sequences [33]. 
Confidence variance measures by describing how much token confidence changes throughout the generated response. It uses the token confidence and examines the changes in order to represent patterns across the response sequence.  The resulting statistical representation will be used in TRACE and will be passed to the feature-fusion stage, where it is combined with the Behavioral View before classification.
3.9 Feature Fusion
Combining the two information from the Behavioral View and Generation-Signal View into one representation on TRACE happens during the Feature Fusion stage. The two views describe different aspects of the same LLM interaction, when combined, it allows TRACE to consider both the observable behavior of the model and its generation patterns when making a detection decision. In the proposed framework, the behavioral representation is combined with the generation-level features consisting of entropy, token confidence, and confidence variance. The resulting fused representation serves as the input to the lightweight TRACE classifier. Through this design, TRACE does not depend on only one type of signal when determining whether a possible backdoor activation has occurred. 
Feature fusion allows the two views to be combined and will help evaluate whether the combined representation provides better detection performance than either the Behavioral View or Generation-Signal View when used individually. 
3.10 Classification Output
The final stage of TRACE is the classification output. In this stage, the Behavioral View and Generation-Signal View are combined through feature fusion, the result is then passed to a lightweight classifier that estimates whether the observed LLM interaction is associated with normal behavior or possible backdoor activation.
To make this decision, TRACE produces a backdoor activation risk score on every interaction whether an observed interaction shows backdoor activation or not. This score serves as the basis for the final classification result of TRACE.  TRACE will use this score to categorize if normal or clean behavior, or as a possible backdoor activation. 
The classification output main goal is to provide a result that can be used for evaluation and monitoring. The risk score allows TRACE to be assessed across different classification thresholds. Meanwhile, the final category provides a simpler indication of the detector’s final decision.

Methodology
This chapter presents the methodology used in design and evaluation of TRACE, including the system design, experimental setup, process, testing procedures, and evaluation methods. The study will also use Llama and Qwen-Instruct models, and configurations from BackdoorLLM for the purpose of controlled experimental conditions. TRACE will analyze the Behavioral View and Generation-Signal View, then these two will be combined and processed by the classifier to produce an activation risk score. The methodology will also evaluate TRACE under seen and unseen trigger conditions. Its performance will be compared with the baseline detection methods and with its individual Behavioral and Generation-Signal View components. This chapter also presents the system requirements, development and testing procedures, evaluation measures, and project development schedule used in conducting the study.
4.1	System Design
The system is mainly designed around the observation that a backdoored LLM may continue to behave normally when the trigger condition is absent, thus an ordinary model evaluation is insufficient for identifying the hidden behavior. Therefore, TRACE examines information available during the interaction with the LLM and the generation-level characteristics to determine if a possible backdoor activation has occurred. 
The system design consists of the two primary processing views, the Behavioral View and the Generation-Signal view. The information from both views is combined through feature fusion and processed by a lightweight classifier to generate activation risk score. 
4.1.1	TRACE System Architecture
TRACE is designed as a lightweight dual-view detection system that analyzes the information produced during the interaction with the target LLM. The system receives the input prompt, and passes it to the selected open-source LLMs. During the response generation, TRACE collects the response and the token probability information that were both produced by the LLM. These outputs are processed through separate views  before being combined for classification.
The first component is the Behavioral View, where the prompt and the response are treated as a single interaction and encoded as text embeddings. This produces a feature representation that summarizes the semantic relationship between the user input and the model's generated behavior. The output of this pathway is prepared as one set of features for the fusion stage. 
In parallel, the second main component is the Generation-Signal View that processes the token-level probability information recorded during the response generation. The key characteristics such as entropy, token confidence, and confidence variance are summarized. These measurements form a separate set of numerical features that describe the model's generation pattern. 
The outputs from the two views are forwarded to the fusion layer, where they are combined into a single feature vector that represents the same LLM interaction from the two sources. Before being forwarded to the classifier the features will be standardized to keep the values within comparable ranges. The fused vector will serve as the final input representation that will be used by the TRACE for detection. 
The fused representation is processed by a lightweight classifier trained using normal and backdoor-activated samples. During inference, the classifier will produce a score representing the likelihood that an interaction is a possible association with a backdoor activation. A decision threshold will be applied to the score to determine the final classification. If the interactions fall below the threshold, it will be treated as normal behavior. While if the interaction exceeds the threshold, it will  be flagged as a possible backdoor activation.

4.1.2	Experimental Design
The study will utilize BackdoorLLM as the main framework for creating controlled backdoor conditions in Llama and Qwen-Instruct models, using BackdoorLLMs available pre-defined attacks, datasets, and configurations. BackdoorLLM will create the experimental backdoor environment, and will be able to provide the backdoored models for TRACE. BackdoorLLM can generate the conditions that this study will be needing, which are the dormant and activated behavior. Since TRACE will be focusing if the detection happened, it can measure whether the model changes behavior after seeing that trigger through BackdoorLLM if the attack successfully activates the backdoor. Moreover, BackdoorLLM contains different trigger architectures, so TRACE will be trained using some trigger configurations and evaluated using different ones from the framework. Lastly, BackdoorLLM is directly relevant to the present study because it provides a standardized experimental environment rather than creating an entirely new backdoor benchmark. BackdoorLLM therefore serves mainly as the benchmark and experimental framework, while TRACE serves as the proposed lightweight detection method that will be tested using backdoor conditions provided by the framework.

As a lightweight detection approach, the system should operate within the reasonable computation requirements. It should avoid depending on another LLM for the main classification process. The system's computational efficiency will later be assessed using detector size and resource requirements. 
TRACE should have a consistent approach across the experimental samples. The same processes should be applied to the training, validation, and testing to avoid differences caused by inconsistencies. Every configuration should also be recorded for reproducibility of the study. In addition, the system should process the selected experimental samples during detection, without altering the behavior of the target LLM. TRACE will observe the two views and use these as inputs to the detector, and will not modify the target model as part of the detection process.
The output of TRACE should be understandable for evaluation. It shall provide a backdoor activation risk score and the monitoring status, so that the result can be examined and tested with different experimental conditions.

4.3	Experimental Evaluation
4.3.1 Experimental Environment and Data Preparation
A base checkpoint of the selected target LLMs will first be prepared and retained as the reference model. There will be separate copies of this checkpoint for the backdoor configurations. For each backdoor configuration, the training dataset will be divided into clean and poisoned samples. The poisoned samples will be modified according to the trigger. Every ratio, output, and parameters will be recorded for every configuration. 
For the researcher-developed configuration, a base model will be used to establish the controlled backdoor condition using a researcher-defined triggers and corresponding target behavior. The configurations will be documented in terms of trigger design, activation condition, target behavior, and training setup. The backdoored model will also be evaluated using inputs with and without the trigger to confirm that the intended behavior will only happen under the specified conditions while retaining its normal performance when the trigger is absent.
The poisoned dataset will be used to fine-tune the copy of the target model. If the original BackdoorLLM implementation requires adaptation for the smaller model, the attack logic and trigger behavior will be preserved and some model parameters will be adjusted within the computation resources
After training, each checkpoint will undergo verification before it is used for TRACE data generation. The clean prompts without triggers will first be submitted in order to monitor whether the model will maintain acceptable normal behavior. Triggered prompts will be used to determine whether the backdoor behavior can be reproduced. Checkpoints that fail to maintain usable clean behavior or even fail to activate the backdoor behavior will not be included in the detector dataset. 
Once the target checkpoints are verified, they will be frozen and used for inference only during the TRACE data collection. For every prompt submitted to the model, the system will record the two views information. The logits recorded during the generation stage will be converted into token probability distributions, through a softmax operation. 
For the experimental controls, the study will include interactions from a backdoored model without the trigger, a backdoored model with the trigger, and when feasible, a clean reference model with and without the same trigger. These conditions will help determine whether TRACE is detecting the actual behavioral change caused by backdoor activation rather than only recognizing the presence of the trigger. Triggered samples that fail to produce the expected backdoor behavior will be recorded separately and will not be labeled as successful activations.
A triggered sample will not automatically be labeled as backdoor-activated. The generated output must first satisfy the expected target behavior of the attack configuration. Samples where the trigger is present but the intended behavior does not occur will be recorded separately so that unsuccessful trigger attempts are not treated as confirmed activation examples.
Before feature extraction, the collected records will undergo preprocessing. Text fields will be checked for missing or invalid outputs, remove duplicates, and exclude incomplete generation records.
The resulting dataset will then be divided into training, validation, and testing groups. The detector-training set will be used to train TRACE while the validation and testing sets will remain separate. Trigger configurations assigned to the unseen-trigger condition will be excluded completely from both training and validation. Their samples will only be introduced during final testing
The validation set will be used for classifier selection, feature-processing decisions, hyperparameter tuning, and threshold calibration. The final test set will remain isolated until the detector configuration has been finalized. 
4.3.2 Behavioral and Generation-Signal Feature Implementation
TRACE will process each LLM interaction through two parallel feature-extraction paths: the Behavioral View and the Generation-Signal View. Both views use the same sample ID so that their outputs can later be matched and fused.
For the Behavioral View, the input prompt and generated response will be formatted as one interaction:
[PROMPT] <input prompt>
[RESPONSE] <generated response>
The formatted text will be passed to a pretrained Sentence-Transformers encoder to generate a fixed-dimensional embedding vector. Contrastive learning will then be applied using only the training data to improve the separation between normal and backdoor-activated behavioral representations. Positive pairs will be constructed from interactions belonging to the same behavioral condition, while negative pairs will consist of interactions from different conditions, particularly normal and backdoor-activated samples. During training, the contrastive-learning process will encourage representations from the same condition to become closer in the embedding space while representations from different conditions become farther apart. The resulting behavioral representation will serve as the behavioral feature vector of the sample.
For the Generation-Signal View, the token-level logits will be converted into probability distributions using the softmax function. If zt,i represents the logit for vocabulary token (i) at generation step (t), its probability is:
pt,i=ezt,ij=1Vezt,j
zt,i  = the logit for token i at time step t 
ezt,j  = exponentiates score
V =  vocabulary size
j=1Vezt,j = Total sum of the exponentiated scores of all possible tokens
pt,i = probability assigned to token i 
Token confidence will be defined as the probability assigned to the token actually generated by the model. For a response containing (T) generated tokens, the mean confidence is: 
C=1Tt=1Tct
Where ct is the confidence of the generated token at step (t). The confidence variance will measure how much token confidence changes throughout the response: 
Var(C)=1Tt=1T(ct-C)2
Entropy will be calculated from the complete token probability distribution at each generation step: 
Ht=-i=1Vpt,i logpt,i 
The mean entropy of the generated sequence will then be computed as: 
H=1Tt=1THt
The Generation-Signal View will produce three primary sequence-level features: mean entropy, mean token confidence, and confidence variance. These features will be standardized using parameters calculated only from the training set, and the same scaling values will be applied to the validation and including the testing data.
At the end of this stage, every interaction will contain a behavioral embedding together with its corresponding generation-signal feature vector. These two representations will then be passed to the feature-fusion and classification stage.
4.3.3 Feature Fusion and Classifier Design
After feature extraction, the behavioral embedding and generation-signal features of each interaction will be matched using their corresponding sample ID. The behavioral embedding contains a higher-dimensional representation of the prompt-response interaction, while the Generation-Signal View produces three numerical features which are mean entropy, mean token confidence, and confidence variance.
Before fusion, the generation-level features will be standardized using the mean and standard deviation. To ensure standardized procedures, the same scaling parameters will be applied to the validation and testing sets. The behavioral embedding will retain its fixed-dimensional representation produced by the selected encoder and will not undergo the same standardization process.
The two feature groups will then be combined through feature concatenation: 
F=[B;G]
(B) represents the behavioral feature vector, (G) represents the standardized generation-signal feature vector, and (F) is the final fused representation. The concatenated vector will then serve as the input to the TRACE classifier.
TRACE will use a lightweight binary classifier that will be trained to distinguish normal and backdoor-activated interactions.
4.3.4 Training and Testing
TRACE will be trained using only the detector-training set. The two views and the contrastive learning process will be developed using this split. The validation set will be used for hyperparameter selection and other calibration. Attack-training data will be used to create the backdoored LLM and the detector-training data will be used to train TRACE. 
The candidate lightweight classifiers, such as logistic regression and a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) will be evaluated using the validation data and the final configuration will be selected based on its detection performance together with computational efficiency. The decision threshold will be selected using the validation set. Different threshold values will be tested, and the one with the highest F1-score will be chosen. The selected threshold will then be used on the test set without changing it.
TRACE pipeline will be frozen and then applied to the test data without doing further tuning. Testing will include both seen-trigger samples and held-out unseen-trigger samples. TRACE will be compared with selected existing detection methods that use similar observable information. ConfGuard [33] will serve as a generation-level baseline because it detects backdoor activation using token-confidence patterns. The semantic-drift approach of Zanbaghi et al. [37] will serve as a behavioral baseline because it uses response embeddings and semantic changes to identify sleeper-agent behavior. In addition, TRACE will be compared with its own Behavioral View and Generation-Signal View used separately through ablation testing.
4.3.5 Evaluation Metrics
A single metric is not sufficient to evaluate a sleeper-agent detector, primarily because trigger-activated behavior is expected to be rare in relation to normal behavior in any realistic evaluation. Under this kind of imbalance, a detector that never flags anything can still post a high accuracy score while catching none of the behavior it was built to detect, so TRACE is evaluated against six metrics for the performance, and three for the detectors operational performance. 
The confusion matrix summarizes TRACE’s classification results based on the actual condition of an interaction and the classification produced by TRACE.

Accuracy is the proportion of all correct predictions. It is reported as a baseline figure for comparability with prior work, but is not treated as a primary metric, since under class imbalance a detector that rarely flags anything can still post a high accuracy score. This will include the True Positive, True Negative, False Positive, and False Negative. 
Accuracy=TP + TNTP + TN + FP + FN

Precision is the number of how many positives were the actual backdoor-activated of all the interactions that TRACE flagged as backdoor-activated. 
Precision=TPTP + FP
Recall is the number on how many TRACE correctly detected out of all the actual backdoor-activated interactions.
Recall=TPTP + FN
F1-Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. It is “a metric used to evaluate the performance of a classification model. It combines precision and recall into a single value and is especially useful when the dataset has imbalanced classes.” [51]
F1=2 Precision  RecallPrecision + Recall
False-Positive Rate (FPR) is the proportion of normal, non-activated interactions that TRACE incorrectly flags as backdoor activations, computed as FPR = FP / (FP + TN). It is used to measure how often the TRACE raises false positives at a selected threshold, which matters because backdoor activation is expected to be rare in comparison to normal model use.
FPR=FPFP + TN
AUROC, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, evaluates the quality of TRACE's underlying risk score independent of any single operating threshold. This metric is meaningful because TRACE outputs a continuous risk score rather than a binary result. It is included to measure how TRACE can identify positive and negative samples.
Inference latency will measure the time TRACE takes to process an interaction and produce a detection result. The measurement will focus on the TRACE detector pipeline, while the text-generation time of the target LLM will be measured separately when possible. Thus, a lower inference latency will result in faster TRACE detection. 
In addition, detector size will measure how large the TRACE model is, while resource usage will measure the memory and computing resources needed during detection.  This will help determine whether the detector remains lightweight compared with larger detection approaches. 
TRACE will be evaluated separately under seen and unseen trigger conditions. For the ablation analysis, Behavioral View, Generation-Signal View, and Full TRACE view, will be evaluated using the same training and testing splits for a standardized and uniform procedure.
